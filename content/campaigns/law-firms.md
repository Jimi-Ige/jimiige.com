## Campaign brief

**Target roles and personas.**
Primary: Managing Partner / Chair (Growth Champion), Executive Director / COO
(Efficiency Driver), Practice Group Leader (Client Advocate). Secondary: CIO /
Director of Innovation (Technology Steward), General Counsel / Ethics & Risk
(Risk Guardian), CFO / Finance Director (Investment Skeptic). Firm profile:
mid-size law firms where partners still supervise the work that carries their
name.

**Core positioning.**
Law firms grow on partner attention, and partner attention does not scale by
hiring. Governed AI moves retrieval, drafting, assembly, and synthesis onto
rails so attorney hours return to positions, clients, and judgment. Improved
operations are the product. AI is the enabling technology. Governance is why
the result can be trusted inside privilege. AI assists operations and
documentation; attorneys retain every legal judgment, every client position,
and every signature. Nothing in this campaign is legal advice.

**Trigger events.**
- A client's outside counsel guidelines arrived with an AI section, and the firm's answer is improvised.
- Associates are underwater on recurring document types while partners are underwater on review, and the pyramid has no slack left.
- A matter budget overran, and the first reliable signal was the pre-bill.
- The firm's best precedent lives in partner heads and matter files nobody can search.
- A client asked, in a pitch or a relationship review, what the firm's AI posture is.
- Leadership pressure to "do something with AI" with no operating answer behind it.

**Primary outcome.**
Booked Workflow Leverage & Trust Assessments with firms matching the
profile. Secondary: playbook readership within target roles.

**Campaign themes.**
1. Grow matter capacity without proportional hiring. 2. Stop redrafting what
the firm has already written. 3. Improve delivery without touching supervision.
4. Govern AI without slowing the firm down.

**Suggested publishing order.**
Rotate themes so no two consecutive posts share one: 01, 04, 07, 10, 02, 05,
08, 11, 03, 06, 09, 12. Two posts per week sustains the rotation for six weeks.

**Conversion path.**
Post → playbook (or persona page for targeted shares) → Workflow Leverage &
Trust Assessment → bounded pilot (two workflows, ninety days).

**Evidence discipline.**
Exact figures in posts come only from claims holding Approved status under
`research/evidence-policy.md`. Verified claims may appear only as attributed
directional statements, with the source name and its caveat adjacent, and
never as an exact figure. Clio's adoption finding (LAW-004) is Verified, not
Approved: it stays directional and always carries its vendor-data label.
Candidate and rejected claims do not appear at all. The section F "social
safe set" in `data/evidence/priority_claims_list.md` predates the WS-19
validation pass and is no longer authoritative on its own. Everything else
stays qualitative. Predictions are always labeled as predictions, and
nothing in any post is legal advice.

### Post 01 - Theme 1: Grow matter capacity without proportional hiring

Every law firm growth plan I have seen lately leans on the same quiet
assumption: that the pyramid scales.

It scales associates. It does not scale the thing that actually constrains
the firm: partners who can win matters, take positions, and sign what leaves
the building. Add associates and you add hours, but you also add the
supervision those hours require, and supervision was the scarce resource to
begin with.

So the useful growth question is not "how many lawyers do we add." It is
"how much of what our partners did this week actually required a partner."
Follow one week of partner time and mark the hours only that partner could
have spent: positions, negotiations, counsel, sign-off. The rest is finding,
assembling, redrafting, and chasing status. That is the capacity hiding in
the firm you already have.

### Post 02 - Theme 1: Grow matter capacity without proportional hiring

The billable hour makes "AI will save time" the least persuasive sentence
you can say in a law firm. The skeptical partners are not wrong.

In an hourly practice, an hour saved is not margin. Unexamined, it is simply
an hour that was not billed. The same hour inside a fixed or capped fee
arrangement is margin directly. Which means the honest capacity conversation
starts with the fee map, not the tool list: where is the book hourly, where
do the alternative fee arrangements live, and where does the firm already
write time off because clients refuse to pay for it.

Saved time is worth the most exactly where the client was never going to pay
for the time. Map that before any pilot, and the economics argue for the
program instead of against it.

### Post 03 - Theme 1: Grow matter capacity without proportional hiring

I will not tell you what share of your associates' hours is recoverable,
because I have not measured your firm.

That is not modesty. It is the method. Numbers borrowed from someone else's
firm make confident slides and bad decisions, and a partnership that senses
one inflated figure discounts the entire program, including the true parts.

The honest alternative fits in a quarter: pick two workflows, one from the
pitch side and one from the matter side. Baseline them for real: first-draft
turnaround on recurring document types, review cycles per document before
sign-off. Run governed pilots with supervision unchanged. Then let your own
numbers make the argument, whichever way they point.

### Post 04 - Theme 2: Stop redrafting what the firm has already written

Ask a partner where the firm's best version of a recurring agreement is. Not
the signed PDF. The thinking: the fallback positions, the negotiated
compromises, the clauses that survived a hard counterparty.

In most firms the honest answer is "in the head of whoever drafted it," plus
a matter file named after a client, findable only by people who already know
it exists. So the next associate rebuilds it, at rates the client may
refuse, slightly worse.

A firm that cannot retrieve its own work product is renting its memory from
whoever happens to remember. Retrieval with provenance, walled by client and
matter, reviewed by an attorney before anything ships: that is not an AI
feature. It is an operating capability AI finally makes practical.

### Post 05 - Theme 2: Stop redrafting what the firm has already written

Associate archaeology is a billing category no firm prints: the hours spent
hunting for the right prior draft before the drafting can start.

The cost hides because it books as ordinary matter time, right up until a
client declines to pay for it and it becomes a write-down instead. The firm
pays for the same learning twice and calls it leverage.

The fix is not discipline. A memo asking lawyers to file exemplars loses to
Friday every time. The fix is making the write-back part of matter close: the
exemplar lands in a curated precedent bank, cleared of confidences, with an
owner and a review date, and the next matter starts from it by default.
Memory as infrastructure, not virtue.

### Post 06 - Theme 2: Stop redrafting what the firm has already written

Reuse has a failure mode no law firm needs described twice: one client's
material surfacing in another client's draft.

Point a model at the whole document management system and that risk now runs
at machine speed. The system holds superseded drafts, positions that lost,
and confidences from every client the firm has ever served. Retrieval does
not know which is which unless the firm tells it.

So the reuse capability that compounds has three properties. Curated
sources, not the pile. Walls by client and matter that mirror the firm's
ethical screens, enforced by the platform rather than by memory. And
provenance on every artifact, so the reviewing attorney sees where each
piece came from before anything leaves the firm. Leverage and trust are the
same build.

### Post 07 - Theme 3: Improve delivery without touching supervision

The fear in every partner meeting about AI is the same fear, differently
dressed: that speed will be bought with the firm's name.

Field evidence says the fear deserves respect. In an experiment at one
elite firm, AI was reported to raise judged quality on tasks inside its
capability frontier, and to lower quality when professionals leaned on it
outside that frontier (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023; consulting tasks at an
elite firm, task-level results, not legal work, and the frontier moves with
the model and the domain). In a law firm, outside the frontier has a
specific shape: the fabricated citation, the legal conclusion nobody
checked against authority.

Here is the design answer. Nothing about supervision changes. The
supervising attorney still reviews position, authority, and risk on
everything that leaves the firm. Every cited authority still gets verified,
by a person, in the source, before anything is filed, because unverified
citations have consequences in front of courts that no efficiency program
can absorb.

What changes is what arrives at review: a draft grounded in the firm's own
vetted work product, with provenance attached, instead of a blank-page
assembly finished at midnight. The signature does not distinguish what the
attorney wrote from what the attorney adopted. A faster draft that passes
the same gate is pure gain. A draft that skips the gate is not capacity.

### Post 08 - Theme 3: Improve delivery without touching supervision

Write-downs are not discovered at the bill. They are only confirmed there.

By the time a pre-bill shocks anyone, the overrun was sitting in time
entries and the matter plan for weeks. Nobody was looking, because looking
meant assembling a spreadsheet by hand, monthly, after the fact.

Matter budgets belong in front of the responsible partner while the phase is
open: budget against actual by phase, drillable to the entries behind it,
with variance escalated to a named partner rather than a distribution list.
Alternative fee arrangements raise the stakes, because on a capped fee the
overrun is not a realization problem later. It is the firm's own money,
leaving in real time.

Synthesis makes this cheap to produce. Traceability is what makes it worth
trusting.

### Post 09 - Theme 3: Improve delivery without touching supervision

The old client-update failure was the update that never went out. The new
one is worse: the update that went out with nobody home.

AI drafts a competent status email in seconds. Send it unread and eventually
it carries a wrong name, a stale number, or a line the client reads twice
for the wrong reason. In a relationship built on counsel, that is corrosive
in a way a late update never was: the client learns that nobody here is
actually paying attention.

The rule I hold in every governed rollout is boring and absolute: an
attorney reads, edits, and owns every client-facing message. No exceptions
for the routine ones, because clients cannot tell which ones you considered
routine. Drafted by a machine is fine. Sent unread is not.

### Post 10 - Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

"Governance will slow the lawyers down" assumes governance is a committee.
It should be rails.

The duties side is already written. ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024)
lists what a lawyer using generative AI must fully consider: competence,
confidentiality, communication, supervision, meritorious claims, candor
toward the tribunal, and reasonable fees. It even sets the competence bar
where operations can reach it: a reasonable understanding of the specific
tool's limits, not AI expertise. (The opinion interprets the ABA Model
Rules; state adoption and opinions vary, and none of this is legal advice.)
What the opinion does not provide is the operating model. That is the
rails' job.

The pattern set for a law firm fits on one page. Client and matter
boundaries that mirror ethical screens. Access that follows matter staffing,
provisioned and revoked with the team. Approved sources: the precedent bank
and the template library, not the whole document store. Provenance that
rides along automatically. Attorney review and verification before anything
leaves the firm. Named sign-out points. Retention rules that honor each
client's guidelines at matter close.

Every one of those buys speed as well as safety. A reviewer who can see
where each piece came from reviews in minutes. A lawyer with a sanctioned
path never needs the shadow one. The firms that move fastest are the ones
whose people never have to stop and wonder whether they are allowed to.

### Post 11 - Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

Clio's Legal Trends research reports that most legal professionals now use
AI in their firms, while a large share say their firm has no AI policy or
they are unaware of one (vendor data from a company that sells practice
technology, with broad definitions of AI; an adoption survey, not causal
proof of anything). Read together, those findings say the use arrived before
the governance did.

Meanwhile your client's outside counsel guidelines may already have an AI
section. The question is whether the firm has an answer per client, or an
improvisation per partner.

The guidelines I have read speak to AI directly: which tools, what
disclosure, what consent, what happens to prompts and outputs when the matter
closes. How common that is across the market is not something I have seen
measured, which is exactly why the answer has to be per client rather than
per market. Where the language is there, the firm's AI posture is no longer
only the firm's decision, and "we are still evaluating" ages badly in a
relationship review.

The move is unglamorous and effective. Inventory the guidelines in force for
AI language. Check each engagement's posture against its own client's terms,
because they do not all say the same thing. Then write the standing answer
the managing partner would happily read aloud. When the general counsel
raises the question in the partner meeting, that answer is the difference
between the program's funeral and its strongest moment.

### Post 12 - Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

You do not need an AI transformation. You need one honest test.

Pick two workflows, one from the client development side and one from the
matter side. Days 1 to 30: baseline them, set the client and matter
boundaries and approved sources, check the affected clients' guidelines,
name the supervising attorneys, and write the verification criteria where
reviewers will see them. Days 31 to 60: run governed pilots with review and
verification unchanged, and instrument the numbers. Days 61 to 90: read the
results against the baseline, codify what worked into defaults, retire what
did not, and brief the partnership on evidence.

Ninety days later the firm knows something true about itself, which is more
than most AI strategies ever deliver. And if the test says stop, you learned
that for the price of a pilot instead of a program.
