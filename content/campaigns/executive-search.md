## Campaign brief

**Target roles and personas.**
Primary: Managing Partner / CEO (Growth Champion), COO / Head of Operations
(Efficiency Driver), Practice Leader / Sector Head (Client Advocate).
Secondary: Head of Technology / Research Operations (Technology Steward),
Risk / Data Privacy Lead (Risk Guardian), CFO / Finance Director (Investment
Skeptic). Firm profile: retained executive search firms where partners still
run searches and the network is the asset.

**Core positioning.**
A search firm sells access, discretion, and assessment judgment, one mandate
at a time. Capacity is consultant attention, research sets the pace of every
search, and the network took a career to build. Governed AI moves market
mapping, drafting, assembly, and reporting onto rails so consultant hours
return to candidates, clients, and judgment. Improved operations are the
product. AI is the enabling technology. Governance is why candidate data,
client confidences, and the read on a person survive the change intact.

**Trigger events.**
- Consultants are maxed on active searches while mandate demand keeps arriving.
- Every search queues behind the same research bench, and the queue sets time to longlist.
- A client asked for faster market coverage than the current bench can produce.
- Clumsy automated outreach burned a relationship somewhere in the market, and people compared notes.
- A client asked how candidate data is protected, and the answer was assembled overnight.
- Leadership pressure to "do something with AI" with no operating answer behind it.

**Primary outcome.**
Booked Workflow Leverage & Trust Assessments with firms matching the
profile. Secondary: playbook readership within target roles.

**Campaign themes.**
1. Run more searches without proportional hiring. 2. Stop remapping markets
the firm already knows. 3. Improve delivery without narrowing the lens.
4. Govern AI without slowing the search down.

**Suggested publishing order.**
Rotate themes so no two consecutive posts share one: 01, 04, 07, 10, 02, 05,
08, 11, 03, 06, 09, 12. Two posts per week sustains the rotation for six weeks.

**Conversion path.**
Post → playbook (or persona page for targeted shares) → Workflow Leverage &
Trust Assessment → bounded pilot (two workflows, ninety days).

**Evidence discipline.**
Quantitative claims in posts come only from the social safe set in
`data/evidence/priority_claims_list.md` (section F), quoted with the caveat
language from `data/evidence/claim_cards.md`. Everything else stays
qualitative. There is no approved primary source for search-firm economics or
AI outcomes in executive search, and no post pretends otherwise: where the
industry evidence is a gap, the post says so or stays qualitative.
Predictions are always labeled as predictions.

### Post 01 - Theme 1: Run more searches without proportional hiring

Every search-firm growth plan I have seen lately carries the same silent
assumption: that the firm can hire proven consultants as fast as it can win
mandates.

It cannot. A consultant who can win a search, run it well, and stand behind
the shortlist arrives with a network that took a career to build, and the
market rarely sells one at a price the model affords. Desks and databases
were never the constraint. Consultant attention is.

So the real growth question is not "how do we hire rainmakers faster." It is
"how much of what our consultants do this week actually requires them."
Follow one desk for a week and mark the hours only that consultant could have
spent. Candidate conversations and references qualify. Market maps rebuilt
from scratch, status updates assembled by hand, and outreach admin do not.
That is the capacity hiding in the firm you already have.

### Post 02 - Theme 1: Run more searches without proportional hiring

Where does the causal evidence actually support AI in search work? Not in
judging people. In the writing and assembly underneath the work.

In a preregistered experiment published in Science in 2023, access to a
general AI assistant cut time on occupation-specific professional writing
tasks by about 40 percent and raised judged quality by about 18 percent,
across roughly 453 college-educated professionals (Noy and Zhang). Those are
controlled task-level results, evaluator-scored, not client mandates and not
executive search, so do not read them as a firm-level number.

But look at a search consultant's week through that lens. Pitch documents,
candidate profiles, client updates, interview summaries, outreach drafts:
the drafting layer is enormous, and none of it is the read on a person. Move
that layer onto governed rails, keep the consultant on every send and every
verdict, and the hours that come back are the hours clients are actually
paying for.

### Post 03 - Theme 1: Run more searches without proportional hiring

Searches active per consultant is the most honest capacity number in a
search firm, and the least examined.

Ask a consultant to carry one more search than they comfortably can, and
nothing visible breaks. Effort holds. What slips first is invisible on any
report: coverage narrows, the update cadence stretches, the patience good
judgment needs quietly leaves the room. The client feels it before the firm
measures it.

I am not going to tell you what your ceiling is, because I have not measured
your firm, and nobody has published a credible benchmark for search-firm
capacity either. That is the point: baseline two workflows for ninety days
and let your own numbers make the argument.

### Post 04 - Theme 2: Stop remapping markets the firm already knows

Ask a search firm where last year's market map of a sector it works
constantly is. Not the placed candidate. The map: who sits where, who moved,
who was approachable and who was off-limits and why.

In most firms the honest answer is "in the head of the researcher who built
it," and that researcher may have left. So the next mandate in the same
territory starts the map again, at research rates, under a client deadline,
slightly worse.

A search firm's edge is what it already knows about its markets. A firm that
cannot retrieve its own maps is renting its memory from whoever happens to
remember. Retrieval with provenance, walled by mandate, verified before use:
that is not an AI feature. It is an operating capability that AI finally
makes practical.

### Post 05 - Theme 2: Stop remapping markets the firm already knows

The most expensive document hunt in a search firm happens during a pitch:
proving a track record the firm already proved.

Somewhere in the files are the mandates that make this pitch credible: the
comparable searches, the time-to-shortlist, the outcomes. Assembling that
proof usually means a partner spelunking through old decks the night before,
and discretion makes it harder, because half the record cannot be named.

The fix is a curated record of prior mandates with an owner, provenance, and
the confidentiality already thought through: what can be said, what can be
described without names, what stays out entirely. The pitch assembles from
it in minutes, and no confidence gets traded for a fee under deadline
pressure.

### Post 06 - Theme 2: Stop remapping markets the firm already knows

Reuse has a sharper edge in search than in any other professional service:
the file is full of things the firm must never carry forward.

What a candidate said in confidence during one mandate. What a client's spec
revealed about a strategy not yet announced. A referee's candor, given on
the understanding it would never be quoted. Point a retrieval system at the
whole archive and every one of those becomes a breach waiting to surface in
the wrong context, at machine speed.

The rule that makes reuse safe is simple to say and worth writing down: keep
the map, never the confidences. Market structure, sector insight, and
process lessons compound across mandates. Conversations do not. Set that
boundary at mandate close, while everyone still remembers which is which.

### Post 07 - Theme 3: Improve delivery without narrowing the lens

The fear in every search partnership about AI is specific: that speed will
be bought with the lens, and the longlist will stop being worth a retained
fee.

The best field evidence says the fear deserves respect. The same experiment
that found large gains when consultants used AI on tasks inside its
capability frontier also found quality fell when they relied on it for tasks
outside that frontier (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023; one elite consulting firm,
task-level results, not a search firm). And where the frontier sits shifts
with the model and the domain, so nobody gets to declare a task safe once
and walk away.

In search, the clearest outside-frontier move is letting a model decide who
leaves the list. Pattern-matching favors the already visible, and an
unvalidated screen can carry adverse impact at scale. The design answer is
routing, not prohibition: machines assemble coverage wide, and a consultant
makes every cut, with the reasons recorded.

### Post 08 - Theme 3: Improve delivery without narrowing the lens

A retained client has bought weeks of work they cannot see. The weekly
update is where they decide whether to believe it.

Most updates are assembled late on Friday from memory and optimism, and it
shows: progress claims nobody could trace, declines quietly omitted, a
stalling search rendered in confident prose. A client can read reassuring
updates for a month before discovering the shortlist date moved.

Rebuild the update from the live record of the search instead: outreach,
responses, conversations, declines, synthesized into a draft the consultant
edits and owns. Report the declines beside the progress, because a spec
meeting market resistance is information the client paid for. An update that
cannot be challenged is not reporting. It is reassurance on a schedule.

### Post 09 - Theme 3: Improve delivery without narrowing the lens

The people a search firm approaches are professionally fluent in automated
flattery. They delete it daily. And they remember who sent it.

One mass-personalized sequence wearing your firm's name, and a relationship
that took years to build reclassifies you as a volume shop. The market
compares notes at exactly the level you operate. The network is the asset,
and clumsy automation is the fastest way ever invented to spend it.

The rule in every governed rollout I run is boring and absolute: a machine
may draft the approach, in the consultant's own register, and only the
consultant may make it. Cap outreach at what a human relationship could
plausibly carry. Automate the scheduling completely, because responsiveness
is the one part of the experience a machine should own. And close every
loop, especially with candidates who do not advance. This year's decline is
next year's client.

### Post 10 - Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the search down

Candidate data is not ordinary business data, and the firms that treat it
that way are one incident from learning the difference in public.

A search file holds the most sensitive fact of a person's working life: that
they would consider a move, and everything they said while considering it.
Much of it concerns people who never asked to be in anyone's database. A
tenant setting does not know which mandate a note belongs to, or that a
referee's candor depends on never being quoted.

The governed answer fits on a page: boundaries at the mandate level, so the
search team defines what the tools reach. Retention written deliberately,
not defaulted: what is kept when the search closes, for how long, and what
is deleted. The verdict human by explicit rule. And a disclosure answer
written before a client asks, because clients have started asking.

### Post 11 - Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the search down

A regulatory fact worth knowing if your firm screens candidates with
software, or advises clients who do: the U.S. EEOC and Department of Justice
have warned that employers' use of AI and automated tools in hiring,
monitoring, pay, or promotion decisions can violate the Americans with
Disabilities Act.

That is a warning and technical assistance, not a finding that any specific
tool is unlawful, and it is not legal advice. Compliance depends on design,
validation, and use. But notice the position a search firm occupies: it
screens candidates in its own process, and it advises clients who screen in
theirs. The exposure runs in both directions.

The operating conclusion is the one good firms already believe for other
reasons: machines may organize and assemble, and a human makes every reject
decision, with the reasons recorded. That control is not compliance theater.
It is also what keeps the longlist worth a retained fee.

### Post 12 - Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the search down

You do not need an AI transformation. You need one honest test.

Pick two workflows, one from winning mandates and one from running searches.
Days 1 to 30: baseline them, approve the research sources, set the mandate
boundaries and retention rules, name the owners, write the disclosure rules
where the team will see them. Days 31 to 60: run governed pilots with
consultant ownership of every outbound word unchanged, and instrument the
measures. Days 61 to 90: read the results against the baseline, codify what
worked into defaults, retire what did not, and brief the partnership on
evidence.

Ninety days later you know something true about your own firm, which is
more than any borrowed benchmark will ever tell you, because in this
industry there is no credible borrowed benchmark. And if the test says stop,
you learned that for the price of a pilot instead of a program.
