## Campaign brief

**Target roles and personas.**
Primary: Managing Partner (Growth Champion), COO / Firm Administrator
(Efficiency Driver), Partner-in-Charge / Practice Leader (Client Advocate).
Secondary: CIO / IT Director (Technology Steward), Quality & Risk Partner
(Risk Guardian), CFO / Finance Partner (Investment Skeptic). Firm profile:
mid-size accounting and CPA firms where partners still review files and sign
them.

**Core positioning.**
A CPA firm sells assurance and advice under a license, through a
preparer-to-reviewer-to-partner hierarchy, on a calendar that decides the year
in a few compressed months. Capacity is not hours worked. It is hours that
survive review and earn a signature, and licensed attention is the thing that
runs out. Governed AI moves retrieval, rollforward, document-chasing, drafting,
and synthesis onto rails so licensed hours return to judgment, clients, and
sign-off. Improved operations are the product. AI is the enabling technology.
Governance is why the result can be trusted with client financial records. One
line governs all of it: AI assists operations and documentation, accountable
licensed professionals retain every judgment and every sign-off, and nothing in
this campaign is tax, accounting, or audit advice.

**Trigger events.**
- Another season ended on overtime and on write-downs that surfaced at billing.
- The growth plan needs experienced staff the hiring market has not supplied for several seasons.
- Reviewers and signing partners are the ceiling while preparation capacity is not.
- The document chase consumed the first six weeks of the engagement calendar again.
- A client asked, in a meeting or in the engagement letter, how the firm uses AI on their work.
- Leadership pressure to "do something with AI" with no operating answer behind it.

**Primary outcome.**
Booked Workflow Leverage & Trust Assessments with firms matching the profile.
Secondary: playbook readership within the six roles above.

**Campaign themes.**
1. Grow the practice without proportional hiring. 2. Stop rebuilding what last
season already produced. 3. Improve delivery without thinning the review
hierarchy. 4. Govern AI without slowing the firm down.

**Suggested publishing order.**
Rotate themes so no two consecutive posts share one: 01, 04, 07, 10, 02, 05,
08, 11, 03, 06, 09, 12. Two posts per week sustains the rotation for six weeks.
Start the run after a deadline wave rather than inside one. Firms read
operating arguments in the months when they can still act on them.

**Conversion path.**
Post to playbook (or persona page for targeted shares) to the Workflow Leverage
& Trust Assessment, then a bounded pilot: two workflows, ninety days, run
between seasons.

**Evidence discipline.**
Exact figures in posts come only from claims holding Approved status under
`research/evidence-policy.md`. Verified claims may appear only as attributed
directional statements, with the source name and its caveat adjacent, and
never as an exact figure. Candidate and rejected claims do not appear at all.
The section F "social safe set" in `data/evidence/priority_claims_list.md`
predates the WS-19 validation pass and is no longer authoritative on its own.
Two of the twelve posts carry a figure: the AICPA staffing ranking (post 01)
and the writing-productivity experiment (post 08). The jagged-frontier quality
finding (post 07) is carried directionally, with its source and caveat
adjacent and no figure attached. Everything else stays qualitative. Two gaps
are stated and never filled: the realization impact of AI drafting is
unmeasured, and no credible mid-market CPA-firm ROI figure exists in the
public record. Predictions are always labeled as predictions, sponsored
surveys carry that label, and nothing in any post is tax, accounting, or audit
advice.

## Posts

### Post 01, Theme 1: Grow the practice without proportional hiring

Every CPA firm growth plan I have read lately rests on the same quiet
assumption: that the firm can hire experienced people fast enough to keep up
with the work it accepts.

The profession has been saying otherwise for years. In the AICPA's 2024
top-issues survey of 667 firms, finding qualified staff ranked as the top issue
for every firm category except sole practitioners, with retaining them second
at the largest sizes (self-reported issue rankings from U.S. firms, not a
measured shortfall, and not an AI finding).

Even that understates it, because staffing is not the binding constraint.
Preparers can be hired. What cannot be hired quickly is the reviewer who
catches what a preparer missed and the partner who signs. Capacity in a CPA
firm is not hours worked. It is hours that survive review and earn a signature.

So the useful growth question is not "how do we hire faster." It is "how much
of what our licensed people did last February actually required a license."
Follow one in-season week of partner and manager time and mark the hours only a
license could have spent. The rest is retrieval, rollforward, formatting, and
chasing documents. That is the capacity already sitting inside the firm you
have.

### Post 02, Theme 1: Grow the practice without proportional hiring

"We will staff up before busy season" is how firms have always grown, and it
quietly prices a bet: that the people hired to prepare will not simply move the
queue further downstream.

They usually do. Add preparers without adding review capacity and the files
arrive faster at the same desks, the review queue lengthens, and the partners
who sign spend the peak deeper in detail than they intended. The ceiling did
not move. It just got a longer line in front of it.

Which is why I ask firms for a number almost nobody tracks: how long a
completed file waits before a reviewer opens it, and how many review cycles it
takes before sign-off. Those two numbers describe the actual ceiling. Headcount
describes the pressure on it.

If the queue is the ceiling, the fix is not more arrivals. It is what arrives:
a file assembled from the prior year and the firm's own approved sources, with
open questions flagged and provenance attached, so review spends its time on
judgment instead of on formatting.

### Post 03, Theme 1: Grow the practice without proportional hiring

I will not tell you what share of your preparation hours is recoverable,
because I have not measured your firm.

That is not modesty, it is the method. Numbers borrowed from someone else's
firm make confident slides and bad decisions, and a partner group that senses
one inflated figure discounts the entire program, including the parts that were
true. In a profession whose product is rigor, the business case is a work
sample.

The honest alternative fits between two seasons. Pick two workflows, one
client-facing and one in production, say the document chase and workpaper
drafting. Baseline them against last season for real: days from acceptance to a
complete file, preparer-to-reviewer turnaround, review notes per engagement,
overtime against plan. Then run governed pilots with the review hierarchy
untouched, and let your own numbers make the argument, whichever way they
point.

If the test says stop, you learned that for the price of a pilot instead of a
program.

### Post 04, Theme 2: Stop rebuilding what last season already produced

Ask a manager where last year's work for a client actually lives. Not the filed
return or the issued report. The thinking: what the client's facts were, why
the treatment went the way it did, what the reviewer questioned and how it was
resolved.

In a lot of firms the honest answer is "in the head of whoever prepared it,"
and that person may be at a different firm now. So the rollforward becomes
archaeology, performed at exactly the point in the calendar with no slack in
it, by someone who was not there the first time.

A firm's prior-year file is the most valuable and least used asset it owns. The
work has already been paid for once. Whether it earns anything the second time
depends entirely on whether the next preparer can find it, trust it, and see
where it came from.

That is what retrieval with provenance is for: prior-year workpapers, memos,
and carryforward items surfacing inside engagement walls, with sources visible,
and every technical citation verified by a licensed professional before
anything in the file relies on it. Not an AI feature. An operating capability
that AI finally makes practical.

### Post 05, Theme 2: Stop rebuilding what last season already produced

The PBC list is the most expensive document in an accounting firm that nobody
thinks about.

It gets rebuilt by hand each year, then grows by discovery in the busiest
weeks. Documents trickle into inboxes and portals. Staff reconcile what arrived
against what was asked for. Seniors chase what did not arrive. Engagements
start weeks late with the file still incomplete, and the whole thing consumes
precisely the weeks when capacity is scarcest.

Rebuilt as a governed workflow, it runs mostly in the background: the complete
request list assembled at acceptance from the engagement type and the
prior-year file, intake logged and matched against what was requested, status
visible to both sides in one place instead of contested across two inboxes, and
reminders drafted for a person to read, edit, and own before they send.

Then measure one thing, by client, season over season: days from engagement
acceptance to a complete file in your hands. It is the rare capacity number
that improves before the season starts rather than after it ends.

### Post 06, Theme 2: Stop rebuilding what last season already produced

Reuse has a failure mode no accounting firm needs described twice: one client's
numbers turning up in another client's file.

Point a retrieval tool at the whole file share and that risk now runs at
machine speed. The share holds superseded schedules, positions that were later
corrected, working files from engagements that ended badly, and the
confidential financial records of every client the firm has ever served.
Retrieval has no idea which is which unless the firm tells it.

So the reuse capability that actually compounds has three properties. Curated
sources with owners and review dates, not the pile. Engagement walls that
mirror staffing, enforced by the platform rather than by memory. Provenance on
every artifact, so the reviewer can see where each piece came from before it
goes into a file that someone will sign.

And one more decision, made before the rollout rather than after it: attest
engagements get the strictest posture in the firm by default. Independence
questions are much cheaper to answer in the design than in the review.

### Post 07, Theme 3: Improve delivery without thinning the review hierarchy

The fear in every partner meeting about AI is the same fear, differently
dressed: that speed will be bought with the signature.

Field evidence says that fear deserves respect. In a field experiment at one
elite global firm, AI was reported to improve performance on tasks inside its
capability frontier and to reduce quality when professionals relied on it for
tasks outside that frontier (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023; consulting tasks at one
elite firm, task-level results, not accounting work, and where the frontier
sits shifts with the model and the domain).

In a CPA firm, outside the frontier has a shape worth naming out loud: a
technical position that reads well and has no authority behind it, and evidence
in a file that looks sufficient and is not. Those are risks the review
hierarchy exists to catch, which is the whole argument for never thinning it.
Nothing here is tax, accounting, or audit advice.

So the design answer is not enthusiasm and not prohibition. Nothing about the
ladder changes: preparer, reviewer, partner, signature. What changes is what
arrives at review, a draft grounded in the firm's own prior-year work with
sources attached instead of a blank workpaper assembled at midnight.

A faster draft that passes the same gate is pure gain. A draft that skips the
gate is not capacity. It is unpriced risk.

### Post 08, Theme 3: Improve delivery without thinning the review hierarchy

There are two reasons review notes per engagement fall, and only one of them is
good news.

They fall because first drafts got better. Or they fall because the reviewer
was saturated in March and started skimming what they would once have rebuilt.
The number alone cannot tell you which, which is why I never let a firm read it
alone.

That first drafts can get better is not speculation. In a preregistered
experiment published in Science, access to a general AI assistant cut time on
occupation-specific professional writing tasks by about 40 percent and raised
judged quality by about 18 percent (Noy and Zhang, Science, 2023; N of about
453; general professional writing, not tax or audit conclusions,
evaluator-scored on experimental outputs, and never measured as realization).

So read the pair. Preparer-to-reviewer turnaround should fall while review
notes fall, and first-pass quality at partner review should hold or improve. If
turnaround improves while first-pass quality slips, the drafts did not get
better, the review got thinner.

And sample-check the cleanest-looking files precisely because they look clean.
Fluency is not evidence.

### Post 09, Theme 3: Improve delivery without thinning the review hierarchy

The old client-communication failure was the reminder that never went out. The
new one is worse: the reminder that went out with nobody home.

An automated chase will send a fourth identical request to a controller who
just lost half her team. It is operationally correct and relationally ruinous,
and the client learns something corrosive from it: nobody over there is
actually paying attention. Trust that took years to build gets spent on a
template.

The rule I hold in every governed rollout is boring and absolute. A person
reads, edits, and owns every client-facing message. No exceptions for the
routine ones, because clients cannot tell which ones you considered routine.
Drafted by a machine is fine. Sent unread is not.

There is a second reason, beyond manners. The schedule that never arrives is
often a signal about the client rather than about the mail: a controller who
left, a system migration nobody mentioned, a business that is having a harder
year than the interim numbers suggest. A tracker sees a missing item. A senior
who reads the silence sees an engagement risk, and sometimes a conversation the
partner should be having.

### Post 10, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

"Governance will slow us down" assumes governance is a committee. It should be
rails.

The pattern set for a CPA firm fits on one page. Engagement boundaries that
decide what AI can reach. Access that mirrors the engagement hierarchy,
provisioned and revoked with staffing. Approved research and template sources,
so drafts are built from the firm's best rather than the file share's oldest.
Provenance that rides along automatically. Licensed review before anything is
client-facing or file-final. Sign-off that keeps its name. Retention rules for
prompts and outputs aligned with the firm's workpaper retention obligations.
Reuse rules set at engagement close, while the boundaries are still fresh.

Every one of those buys speed as well as safety. A reviewer who can see where
each piece came from reviews in minutes instead of rebuilding. A senior with a
sanctioned path never goes looking for the shadow one. The firms that move
fastest on this are the ones whose people never have to stop and wonder whether
they are allowed to.

One line sits above the whole apparatus, and it is the reason clients can live
with any of it: AI assists operations and documentation, and the accountable
licensed professional retains every judgment and every sign-off.

### Post 11, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

Somewhere in your next partner meeting about AI, the quality and risk partner
is going to ask the confidentiality question. The program that survives is the
one that prepared the answer before the meeting.

Six chairs decide anything real in a CPA firm: the managing partner, the COO or
firm administrator, the partners-in-charge, the CIO, the quality and risk
partner, and the CFO. Each one asks a different question, and a champion-led
initiative dies because it answers one of them brilliantly and meets the other
five unprepared.

In this profession two of those questions need answers written down in advance.
What happens to client financial records inside these tools, at the engagement
level rather than the tenant level. And what an attest engagement requires that
the rest of the firm does not, decided as a default rather than discovered as
an exception.

Then write the standing client answer, the one the managing partner would
happily read aloud, and give the quality and risk partner a genuine design
seat. Controls added at the end read as concessions. Controls designed in read
as competence, which is the same thing the firm sells.

### Post 12, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

You do not need an AI transformation. You need one honest test, and in this
profession you need to run it between seasons.

Pick two workflows, one client-facing and one in production. Days 1 to 30:
baseline them against last season, set the engagement boundaries and approved
sources, name the accountable owners, and write the quality criteria where
reviewers will actually see them. Days 31 to 60: run governed pilots with the
review hierarchy unchanged, instrument the numbers, and hold a short weekly
read of what the numbers and the people are both saying. Days 61 to 90: read
the results against the baseline, codify what worked into defaults and reuse
rules, retire what did not, and brief the partnership on evidence.

The timing is not decoration. A firm that changes its production system in the
quiet months has a proven system before the deadline wave. A firm that
announces one in November runs the old system and the new system at once, in
the weeks with no slack to reconcile them, and the season decides the
program's reputation before the program was ready.

Ninety days later you know something true about your own firm, which is more
than most AI strategies ever deliver.
