## Campaign brief

**Roles and personas.**
Primary: CEO / Managing Director (Growth Champion), COO / Delivery Director
(Efficiency Driver), Practice Lead / Solution Director (Client Advocate).
Secondary: CTO / CIO (Technology Steward), Security & Risk Lead (Risk
Guardian), CFO / Finance Director (Investment Skeptic). Firm profile:
mid-market IT consulting and systems-integration firms where senior engineers
and architects still scope, estimate, and review the work.

**Core positioning.**
An IT consulting firm sells engineered outcomes against fixed bids and T&M
rates. Margin is decided at the estimate, defended at the review gate, and lost
on the bench, and the scarce resource in all three places is the same senior
engineer. Governed AI moves drafting, retrieval, and assembly onto rails so
senior technical attention returns to scoping, pricing, and standing behind
what ships. Improved operations are the product. AI is the enabling technology.
Governance is why the result can be trusted inside a client's environment.

**Trigger events.**
- Two fixed bids overran and nobody can say from the data which part of the estimate was wrong.
- The same integration, migration, or environment build keeps getting reconstructed from scratch.
- Roll-off dates arrive as a surprise and the bench forms before the pipeline can absorb it.
- A client's MSA or security questionnaire arrived with an AI section, and the firm's answer varies by who is asked.
- Coding and drafting assistants are already in use across project teams, inside client environments, with no shared control model.
- Leadership pressure to "do something with AI" with no operating answer behind it.

**Primary outcome.**
Booked Workflow Leverage & Trust Assessments with firms matching the profile.
Secondary: playbook readership among the roles named above.

**Campaign themes.**
1. Deliver more projects without proportional hiring. 2. Stop rebuilding what
the firm already shipped. 3. Raise throughput without diluting engineering
quality. 4. Govern AI without slowing delivery down.

**Suggested publishing order.**
Rotate themes so no two consecutive posts share one: 01, 04, 07, 10, 02, 05,
08, 11, 03, 06, 09, 12. Two posts per week sustains the rotation for six weeks.

**Conversion path.**
Post → playbook (or persona page for targeted shares) → Workflow Leverage &
Trust Assessment → bounded pilot (two workflows, ninety days).

**Evidence discipline.**
Quantitative claims in posts come only from the social safe set in
`data/evidence/priority_claims_list.md` (section F), quoted with the caveat
language from `data/evidence/claim_cards.md`. Three of the twelve posts carry a
number and the rest stay qualitative, including the adoption-versus-scale
pattern, which is carried as a pattern rather than as percentages because the
safe set covers the pattern and not the underlying figures. The consulting
field experiment always carries its elite-firm limit, and the support-agent
study is always labeled a transfer hypothesis for service-desk-like work rather
than proof for engineering delivery. Two gaps are stated rather than filled: there is no
public causal study of AI's return for mid-market IT consulting firms, and the
effect of AI assistance on estimate accuracy is unmeasured. No invented
clients, results, or payback periods.

### Post 01, Theme 1: Deliver more projects without proportional hiring

Every IT consulting growth plan I read has the same silent assumption buried
in it: that the firm can hire senior engineers and architects as fast as it can
win work.

It cannot. The person who can read a requirement, size the work, choose the
architecture, and stand behind what ships takes years to make and a fortune to
buy. Developers were never the constraint. Senior technical attention is.

Which means the growth question is not "how do we hire faster." It is "how much
of what our architects did this week actually required an architect." Follow one
week of senior technical hours and mark the ones only that person could have
spent. The rest is assembly: hunting for the last solution outline, reformatting
a response, redrawing a diagram, writing the handover document the budget
already ran past. That is the capacity hiding inside the firm you already have.

### Post 02, Theme 1: Deliver more projects without proportional hiring

Count the senior hours your firm spent on pursuits last quarter. Now count how
many of them were billable.

Pursuit-to-SOW is where an IT consulting firm's margin is decided, and it runs
almost entirely on the people delivery cannot spare: the RFP response, the
solution outline, the estimate, the SOW language, the technical review before
anything is promised. Most of those hours are not the estimate. They are
assembly around the estimate.

The drafting layer is where the causal evidence is strongest. In a preregistered
experiment with roughly 453 college-educated professionals, access to a general
AI assistant cut time on mid-level professional writing tasks by about 40
percent and raised evaluator-scored quality by about 18 percent (Noy and Zhang,
Science, 2023). Read the limits carefully: controlled, occupation-specific
writing tasks, not client engagements, not engineering work, and not estimates.

So split the workflow. Let the assembly move. Keep the bid call and the estimate
exactly where they are, in front of a senior engineer who has to live with them.
Nobody has measured what AI assistance does to estimate accuracy, which is
reason enough not to find out on a fixed bid.

### Post 03, Theme 1: Deliver more projects without proportional hiring

Bench time is the most expensive number in an IT consulting firm that almost
nobody forecasts.

Every firm tracks it after the fact. Few see it coming. Roll-off dates live in
one system, the pipeline lives in another, and the two get reconciled in
somebody's head about a week before three engineers become overhead. Then a
fixed bid somewhere quietly pays for them.

That is not a hiring problem or a sales problem. It is a signal problem. The
firm already holds every fact it needs, in project plans, staffing records, and
the pipeline, and nobody has put them on one spine where a delivery director can
read them together on a Tuesday instead of assembling them on a Friday.

I am not going to tell you what your recoverable bench is, because I have not
measured your firm. That is the whole point. Baseline it for ninety days and let
your own numbers make the argument.

### Post 04, Theme 2: Stop rebuilding what the firm already shipped

Ask a delivery lead where the firm's best data-migration pattern lives. Not the
final deliverable. The thinking: the sequencing, the cutover plan, the two
assumptions that turned out to be wrong.

In most firms the honest answer is "in the head of whoever built it," and that
engineer may be at a competitor now. So the next project rebuilds it, at senior
rates, on a fixed bid, slightly worse.

An IT consulting firm's edge is what it has already engineered. A firm that
cannot retrieve its own delivered work is renting its memory from whoever
happens to remember. Retrieval with provenance, walled by client and contract,
reviewed before reuse: that is not an AI feature. It is an operating capability
that AI finally makes practical.

### Post 05, Theme 2: Stop rebuilding what the firm already shipped

The most valuable asset in your firm is what your best engineer knows about
every system the firm has delivered, and it walks out of the building every
evening.

The closest causal evidence for encoding that knowledge does not come from
consulting at all. In a staggered deployment of a generative AI assistant
across 5,179 customer-support agents, issues resolved per hour rose by about 14
percent on average, and by about 34 percent for novice and lower-skilled
workers, with minimal gains for the most skilled (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond,
NBER w31161, 2023).

Label that honestly. The setting is software customer-support chat, not project
delivery. For service-desk-like and managed-services work inside an IT
consulting firm it is a transfer hypothesis, never direct proof, and it says
nothing about architecture or estimation.

The shape of the result is what earns the second read, and what follows is my
reading of it rather than a finding of the study. The workers who gained most
were the ones with the least accumulated experience, which is what you would
expect if the value sits in making hard-won knowledge available where the work
actually happens. Read that way, it is a curation and documentation problem
before it is an AI problem, and most firms have never done the first part.

### Post 06, Theme 2: Stop rebuilding what the firm already shipped

Reuse has a failure mode nobody puts in the capability deck: shipping one
client's property into another client's system.

Point an assistant at the firm's whole delivery history and that risk now runs
at machine speed. The history holds code the firm does not own, components a
contract left behind with a client, patterns superseded two platform versions
ago, and generated snippets nobody traced. Retrieval cannot tell which is which
unless the firm tells it.

So the reuse capability that actually compounds has three properties. Curated
sources with owners, review dates, and IP status, not the whole repo history.
Walls by client and contract, so what one engagement built stays with that
engagement. Provenance on every component, reused or generated, so a reviewer
can see where each piece came from before it merges.

A component you cannot trace is a liability you have already shipped.

### Post 07, Theme 3: Raise throughput without diluting engineering quality

The fear in every delivery leadership meeting about AI is the same fear,
differently dressed: that speed gets bought with quality, and on a fixed bid the
firm pays for it twice.

The best field evidence says both halves of that fear are real. In a field
experiment with 758 consultants at one elite firm, AI use on tasks inside its
capability frontier meant 12.2 percent more tasks completed, 25.1 percent
faster, at more than 40 percent higher judged quality than the control group,
and quality fell when consultants relied on it outside that frontier (Dell'Acqua
et al., 2023). The limits are part of the claim: one elite global firm,
consulting-style knowledge tasks, task-level results, not engineering delivery
and not mid-market ROI.

The design conclusion is neither enthusiasm nor prohibition. It is routing plus
review.

Which means nothing about your gates changes. Design review still happens before
the architecture is committed. Code review still happens before anything merges.
A senior engineer is still accountable for what ships, whoever or whatever
produced it. What changes is what arrives at the gate: work grounded in the
firm's own reviewed patterns, marked with its provenance, instead of a
blank-page build finished at midnight.

Review discipline is not the tax on throughput. It is what makes the throughput
sellable.

### Post 08, Theme 3: Raise throughput without diluting engineering quality

If you raise delivery throughput without raising review capacity, you have not
raised capacity. You have raised exposure.

This is the arithmetic most firms skip. Assisted work reaches the design and
code gates faster, and it arrives fluent, which is the hard part, because fluent
work reads finished. A gate that approves fluent work unread is not a gate. It
is a formality with a senior engineer's name on it, and on a fixed bid the firm
owns every defect it lets through.

Three things keep the gate real. Name the accountable engineer before the work
arrives, not after. Budget review time as the scarce resource it is, and protect
it first when throughput rises rather than trimming it first. And mark
AI-assisted work as such, with its provenance, so reviewers know what they are
reading and read it accordingly.

The client never asks what generated the code. They ask who reviewed it.

### Post 09, Theme 3: Raise throughput without diluting engineering quality

On a fixed bid, the month a project spends quietly green is the month the margin
leaves.

Synthesis helps here, and synthesis is also the trap. A model summarizing status
reports will smooth a slipping project into confident prose, and confident prose
is exactly what a steering committee cannot challenge. The failure is not the
summary. It is a reporting chain where no number can be traced, so no number can
be argued with.

The fix is boring and structural. One source of record per metric. Synthesis
from delivery artifacts, never from prior summaries. Burn read against the
estimate weekly, in front of someone who can act on it. Risk escalated to a
named owner with a date, never to a distribution list. Every portfolio number
drillable to the artifact behind it in one step.

A status number you cannot trace is a rumor wearing a format.

### Post 10, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing delivery down

"Governance will slow delivery down" assumes governance is a committee. In an IT
consulting firm it should be rails.

The pattern set fits on one page. Client and project boundaries that decide what
AI can reach. Access that mirrors project staffing, provisioned and revoked with
it. Approved tools and approved sources. Credentials out of prompts, absolutely.
Provenance riding along on everything generated or reused. Review gates before
anything merges. Named approval points. Retention and reuse rules written at
project close, while the contract boundaries are still fresh.

Every one of those buys speed as well as safety. A reviewer who can see where
each piece came from reviews in minutes. An engineer with a sanctioned path
never goes looking for the other one. And this industry carries a reason to get
it right that other industries do not: a firm that implements AI for clients
cannot be found running it ungoverned at home. Your own governance posture is a
work sample.

### Post 11, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing delivery down

Licenses issued is the most flattering AI metric in a consulting firm, and the
least informative.

The enterprise surveys make the point better than I can, and the pattern across
them matters more than any single figure. Regular AI use is now widespread,
most organizations have still not scaled it past experiments, and only a
minority report any enterprise-level earnings impact at all (McKinsey State of
AI 2025; Deloitte generative-AI survey 2025). Those are self-reported,
large-enterprise-skewed consultancy surveys produced by firms that sell AI
services, they are not IT-consulting figures, and they are not audited financial
impacts. Read them for one thing only: access is not value capture.

For this industry the honest scoreboard is six numbers. Estimate-to-actual
variance by project type. Proposal and SOW cycle time. Share of delivered work
built on reviewed, reusable components. Defects and rework caught at the gates
versus in production. Bench time between assignments. Margin by project, visible
during delivery rather than at close.

And the gap, stated instead of filled: no public causal study measures AI's
return for a mid-market IT consulting firm, and nobody has measured what AI
assistance does to estimate accuracy. Your own baseline is not a nice-to-have.
It is the evidence.

### Post 12, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing delivery down

You do not need an AI transformation. You need one honest test.

Pick two workflows, one from the pursuit side and one from delivery. Days 1 to
30: baseline them, estimate variance and cycle times included, set the client
and project boundaries and the approved tools, name the accountable engineer at
every gate, and write the quality criteria where reviewers will see them. Days
31 to 60: run governed pilots with the review gates unchanged, instrument the
numbers, and hold a short weekly read of what the data and the engineers are
both saying. Days 61 to 90: read the results against the baseline, codify what
worked into defaults and reuse rules, retire what did not, and brief leadership
on evidence instead of enthusiasm.

Ninety days later you know something true about your own firm, which is more
than most AI strategies ever deliver. And if the test says stop, you learned
that for the price of a pilot instead of a program.
