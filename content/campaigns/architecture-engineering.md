## Campaign brief

**Target roles and personas.**
Primary: Managing Principal / President (Growth Champion), COO / Director of
Operations (Efficiency Driver), Studio / Discipline Leader (Client Advocate).
Secondary: CIO / Digital Practice Leader (Technology Steward), Risk Manager /
QA-QC Director (Risk Guardian), CFO / Finance Director (Investment Skeptic).
Firm profile: mid-size architecture and engineering firms where the people who
seal the work still touch the work.

**Core positioning.**
A&E firms sell designed and sealed work through project teams, against fees
set early and spent late. The binding constraint is the attention of licensed
professionals, and the seal does not scale by hiring in this market. Governed
AI moves assembly, drafting, retrieval, and synthesis onto rails so licensed
attention returns to design judgment, checking, and the client. Improved
operations are the product. AI is the enabling technology. Governance is why
the result can be trusted in a sealed set. AI assists production and
documentation; licensed architects and engineers retain responsible charge,
every design decision, and the seal.

**Trigger events.**
- Backlog at or near record levels while key roles stay open for months.
- SOQs and proposals consuming principal weekends to assemble material the firm already has.
- RFI volume on a recent project traced back to document quality, and the margin that went with it.
- Fee burn discovered at 80 percent with the construction documents at 50.
- An owner's RFQ or contract asked how the firm uses AI, and the answer was improvised.
- Leadership pressure to "do something with AI" with no operating answer the QA/QC director will accept.

**Primary outcome.**
Booked Workflow Leverage & Trust Assessments with firms matching the
profile. Secondary: playbook readership within target roles.

**Campaign themes.**
1. Grow without proportional hiring. 2. Stop recreating work.
3. Improve delivery without lowering quality. 4. Govern AI without slowing the firm down.

**Suggested publishing order.**
Rotate themes so no two consecutive posts share one: 01, 04, 07, 10, 02, 05,
08, 11, 03, 06, 09, 12. Two posts per week sustains the rotation for six weeks.

**Conversion path.**
Post → playbook (or persona page for targeted shares) → Workflow Leverage &
Trust Assessment → bounded pilot (two workflows, ninety days).

**Evidence discipline.**
Quantitative claims in posts come only from the social safe set in
`data/evidence/priority_claims_list.md` (section F), quoted with the caveat
language from `data/evidence/claim_cards.md`. Everything else stays
qualitative. Predictions are always labeled as predictions, association
sentiment surveys are labeled as sentiment surveys, and nothing in any post
reads as engineering or architectural advice.

### Post 01, Theme 1: Grow without proportional hiring

The strangest number in the built environment right now is not on any
drawing. It is the gap between the work firms have won and the hands they
have to deliver it.

Engineering firm leaders surveyed in Q1 2026 reported a median backlog of 11
months, with 47 percent of firms at a year or more, and 88 percent still
carrying at least one open position (ACEC Research Institute, N=628; a member
sentiment survey, not a census, and backlog measures demand, not delivery
efficiency). Record demand, empty seats.

You cannot hire your way out of that market, because everyone is trying to
hire out of the same pool. The honest question is different: how much of what
your licensed people do this week actually requires a license? Follow one
week of principal hours. The design judgment, the checking, the client calls,
that is the product. The assembling, formatting, hunting, and re-solving
around it is the capacity hiding in the firm you already have.

### Post 02, Theme 1: Grow without proportional hiring

The fee on a design project is set early, before the unknowns surface, and
spent late, in the construction documents, where the hours actually live.
That asymmetry runs the economics of every A&E firm I have worked with.

It also points at the growth lever nobody budgets for. Between concept and
sealed set, a surprising share of hours goes to work the firm has already
done: the detail redrawn from memory, the spec section rebuilt instead of
retrieved, the basis-of-design narrative written from scratch for the tenth
time. None of that is design judgment. All of it competes with design
judgment for the same fee.

Move that assembly work onto governed, reusable rails and the same team
delivers more of the backlog, without a single additional hire and without
touching the checking discipline. That is what "grow without proportional
hiring" actually means in a design firm. It is an operations program, not a
software purchase.

### Post 04, Theme 2: Stop recreating work

Somewhere in your firm is a detail that has kept water out of ten buildings.

Ask a project team to find it and you will learn how the firm actually
stores its knowledge: in the heads of the people who drew it, some of whom
have retired. So the team redraws it, at production rates, on a deadline,
slightly differently. Sometimes the difference is an improvement. Sometimes
it is the RFI storm of 2027, waiting in the set.

A design firm's edge is what it has already solved. A firm that cannot
retrieve its own proven work is renting its memory from whoever happens to
remember. Retrieval with provenance and performance history, walled by
client and project, reviewed at QA/QC before anything is issued: that is not
an AI feature. It is an operating capability that AI finally makes
practical.

### Post 05, Theme 2: Stop recreating work

Watch what actually happens when an RFQ lands. Principals and senior staff
spend evenings hunting project sheets out of old submittals, updating
resumes that are six versions stale, and rewriting past-performance
narratives the firm has written many times before. The firm is assembling
proof of work it already did, at its most expensive rates.

The drafting layer is where the causal evidence is strongest. In a
preregistered experiment published in Science, access to a general AI
assistant cut time on professional writing tasks by about 40 percent while
raising judged quality by about 18 percent (Noy & Zhang, 2023, N of about
453; task-level results on general professional writing, not design work,
and not firm-level ROI). SOQ narratives and project descriptions sit close
to that evidence. Sealed drawings do not, and the playbook is explicit about
the difference.

The operating change: a curated qualifications library with an owner and
provenance, assembly on rails, and principal review focused on the three
calls that matter: chase or pass, what to promise, what fee the work needs.

### Post 06, Theme 2: Stop recreating work

Reuse has a failure mode nobody puts on the slide: point a model at the
whole project archive and it will faithfully surface the superseded detail,
the spec section from a different code cycle, and one client's confidential
project data inside another client's set.

Retrieval does not know which detail is proven and which one caused the
claim. The archive contains both, and they look identical to a model.

So the reuse capability that actually compounds has three properties.
Curated libraries, not the pile: approved details and master specs with
owners and review dates. Boundaries, so client and project walls hold at
machine speed. Provenance, so the checker at QA/QC can see where every
component came from and what it has survived before the set goes out.

Leverage and trust are the same build. In sealed work they were never
separable.

### Post 07, Theme 3: Improve delivery without lowering quality

The fear in every principals' meeting about AI is the same fear, differently
dressed: that throughput will be bought with document quality, and the seal
will pay for it.

The best field evidence says the fear has a real edge. In an experiment with
758 consultants at one elite firm, AI raised judged quality by more than 40
percent on tasks inside its capability frontier, and lowered quality when
people leaned on it for tasks outside that frontier (Dell'Acqua et al.,
2023; consulting tasks at one elite firm, and the transfer to engineering
work is a caution, not a measurement).

For a design firm the outside-frontier examples are not hypothetical:
calculations and code-compliance assertions that no qualified professional
has verified. The design answer is routing plus verification, not enthusiasm
or prohibition. Drafting and assembly move onto rails. Calculations and
code conclusions stay with licensed professionals, full stop. Nothing about
QA/QC changes except what arrives at the gate: a draft with provenance
instead of a blank-page assembly finished at midnight.

### Post 08, Theme 3: Improve delivery without lowering quality

RFI volume is the delayed invoice for document quality. The set that left
the studio thin comes back six months later as questions, change orders, and
a contractor who has learned to price your documents accordingly.

Almost no firm closes that loop. The RFIs get answered project by project,
the lessons stay in the inbox of whoever answered them, and the next set
carries the same weaknesses out the door.

Two operating changes close it. First, trace last year's RFIs back to their
documents and put a number on what document quality is costing during
construction; that number funds the program. Second, make the write-back a
closing deliverable with an owner: what the RFIs taught, filed into the
detail and spec libraries where the next project will find it by default.
Memory as infrastructure, not virtue.

### Post 09, Theme 3: Improve delivery without lowering quality

The engineering profession has already drawn the line on AI, and it drew it
in a sensible place.

NSPE's Board of Ethical Review, in Case 24-2, concluded two things at once:
AI-assisted report drafting can be ethical when a competent engineer
thoroughly reviews and verifies the content under the engineer's direction
and control, and uploading client confidential information to an open AI
interface without consent is unethical, treated as exposing it to the
public domain. (A hypothetical ethics case interpreting the NSPE Code, not
a court decision, and not legal advice.)

Notice what that line is not. It is not "no AI." It is direction, control,
thorough verification, and consent: the same responsible-charge logic the
profession has always run on, applied to a new production tool. Firms that
operationalize those four words, in the quality manual, in the tool list, in
the data boundaries, get the leverage and keep the seal clean. Firms that
improvise get whichever outcome luck assigns them.

### Post 10, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

"Governance will slow the studio down" assumes governance is a committee. It
should be rails.

The pattern set for a design firm fits on one page: project-level boundaries
that decide what AI can reach. Role-based access that mirrors the project
team. Approved detail and spec libraries that decide what drafts are built
from. Provenance that rides along automatically. QA/QC review before
anything is issued. Named approval points, so releasing a set has an owner.
Retention and reuse rules settled at project close, while everyone still
remembers what belongs to whom.

Every one of those buys speed as well as safety. A checker who can see where
each component came from checks in minutes. A designer with a sanctioned
path never needs the shadow one. The firms moving fastest on AI are not the
ones with the fewest controls. They are the ones whose people never have to
stop and wonder whether they are allowed to.

### Post 11, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

Somewhere in your next principals' meeting about AI, the QA/QC director is
going to ask the liability question. The program that survives is the one
that prepared the answer before the meeting.

Six chairs hold a veto on any real change in a design firm: the managing
principal, the operations director, the studio and discipline leaders, the
digital practice leader, the risk and QA/QC chair, and the CFO. Each asks a
different question. Growth without dilution. Delivery reliability. The
quality of work that carries the seal. One governed platform, not another
tool on the pile. Liability, document quality, confidentiality. Cost and
return. Champion-led AI initiatives die because they answer one chair
brilliantly and meet the other five unprepared.

Design for all six from the start, and give the QA/QC chair a seat at the
design table rather than a review at the end. Controls added late read as
concessions. Controls designed in read as competence, and competence is what
gets a room of licensed professionals to yes.

### Post 12, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

You do not need an AI transformation. You need one honest test that the
seal-holders in your firm can live with.

Pick two workflows, one from pursuit and one from production. Days 1 to 30:
baseline them honestly, set the project boundaries and approved libraries,
name the owners, and write the QA/QC criteria for AI-assisted content where
the checkers will see them. Days 31 to 60: run governed pilots with checking
discipline unchanged, and instrument the numbers. Days 61 to 90: read the
results against the baseline, codify what worked into defaults and library
rules, retire what did not, and brief the principals on evidence.

Ninety days later you know something true about your own firm, which is
more than most AI strategies ever deliver. The seal never moved, the
checking never thinned, and if the test says stop, you learned that for the
price of a pilot instead of a program.
