## Campaign brief

**Target roles and personas.**
Primary: CEO / Owner (Growth Champion), COO / Service Delivery Director
(Efficiency Driver), vCIO / Account Management Lead (Client Advocate).
Secondary: CTO / Automation Lead (Technology Steward), Security & Compliance
Lead (Risk Guardian), CFO / Finance Director (Investment Skeptic). Firm
profile: mid-size MSPs where the owner still knows the top clients by name and
senior engineers still touch tickets.

**Core positioning.**
An MSP sells outcomes per seat at a fixed monthly fee, so every saved
engineer-hour is margin and every recurring ticket is a leak. Governed AI moves
runbook retrieval, ticket documentation, QBR assembly, and quote drafting onto
rails so engineer hours return to the escalations and the client relationships
that actually need them. Improved operations are the product. AI is the
enabling technology. Governance is why the client keeps trusting the firm with
the keys. An accountable engineer stays on every consequential action; AI
never holds standing permission to change a client environment.

**Trigger events.**
- A senior engineer gave notice, and half the runbook knowledge is in their head.
- Tier-one escalations are consuming tier-three time, and the after-hours rotation is fraying.
- QBR packs get assembled the night before from ticketing exports, and a renewal wobbled.
- A client's security questionnaire asked how the MSP uses AI inside their environment.
- The same tickets keep recurring from the same clients while the agreement price stays fixed.
- Leadership pressure to "do something with AI" with no operating answer behind it.

**Primary outcome.**
Booked Workflow Leverage & Trust Assessments with firms matching the
profile. Secondary: playbook readership within target roles.

**Campaign themes.**
1. Grow the client base without proportional engineering hires. 2. Stop
re-solving tickets the desk already solved. 3. Improve delivery without
loosening control. 4. Govern AI without slowing the desk down.

**Suggested publishing order.**
Rotate themes so no two consecutive posts share one: 01, 04, 07, 10, 02, 05,
08, 11, 03, 06, 09, 12. Two posts per week sustains the rotation for six weeks.

**Conversion path.**
Post → playbook (or persona page for targeted shares) → Workflow Leverage &
Trust Assessment → bounded pilot (two workflows, ninety days).

**Evidence discipline.**
Quantitative claims in posts come only from the social safe set in
`data/evidence/priority_claims_list.md` (section F), quoted with the caveat
language from `data/evidence/claim_cards.md`. Everything else stays
qualitative. The support-agent productivity study is always labeled a transfer
hypothesis, never MSP proof. No MSP margin, utilization, or seat-economics
benchmarks are cited anywhere, because no approved primary source exists;
where the number would go, the gap is stated instead.

### Post 01, Theme 1: Grow the client base without proportional engineering hires

Every MSP growth plan I have seen lately carries the same silent assumption:
that each tranche of new seats can be matched with engineers hired at a price
the per-seat fee can absorb.

It cannot, not reliably. The engineers who can untangle a hard escalation and
design the fix that stops a ticket from coming back are scarce, expensive,
and slow to grow. Seats and endpoints were never the constraint. Senior
engineering attention is.

Which means the real growth question is not "how do we hire faster." It is
"how much of what our senior engineers do this week actually requires them."
Follow one week of senior hours and mark the ones only that engineer could
have spent. The rest is re-solving solved tickets, rebuilding QBR packs by
hand, and onboarding clients from memory. That is the capacity hiding in the
desk you already have.

### Post 02, Theme 1: Grow the client base without proportional engineering hires

The best causal evidence for AI on ticket work comes from the closest setting
anyone has studied properly: customer support.

In a staggered deployment across 5,179 support agents, a generative AI
assistant raised issues resolved per hour by about 14 percent on average, and
by about 34 percent for novice and lower-skilled agents, with minimal gains
for the most experienced (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, NBER working paper
31161). The caveat matters as much as the number: that was single-tenant
software support chat, agents could ignore the suggestions, and it is not
proof of anything about multi-tenant MSP work with SLAs and privileged
access. For an MSP it is a transfer hypothesis, not a result.

But look at the shape of the finding. The assistant worked by making the
accumulated knowledge of the best agents available to everyone else. That is
exactly what an MSP runbook corpus is supposed to do, and mostly does not.
The honest move is not to bank the 14 percent. It is to baseline your own
desk and test whether the shape holds.

### Post 03, Theme 1: Grow the client base without proportional engineering hires

Here is a number I will not give you: the industry-average MSP margin per
seat.

Not because it would not be useful. Because I have never found a primary
source for it that survives scrutiny: open methods, a stated sample, no
vendor thumb on the scale. Most of the seat-economics benchmarks that
circulate in this industry trace back to webinars and marketing decks, and a
benchmark without methods is a rumor with confidence.

What works instead is cheaper and more honest: price each agreement against
the effort it actually consumes. Seats per engineer, recurring-ticket rate
per client, effort versus agreement value. Three numbers from your own stack,
read quarterly, will tell you which agreements are quietly underwater, and no
industry average can tell you that at all.

### Post 04, Theme 2: Stop re-solving tickets the desk already solved

A senior engineer at an MSP gives notice. Everyone thinks about coverage:
who takes the after-hours phone, who owns the big client.

The real loss is quieter. It is the runbook that only existed in their head:
the quirk in that client's environment, the fix that worked last time, the
thing you never touch on that server and the reason why. Six months of
tickets will now take longer to resolve, and nobody will attribute a single
one of them to the resignation.

Tribal knowledge is a single point of failure with a notice period. The fix
is not a documentation sprint, those die on the first busy Monday. It is
making the write-back part of closing the ticket: the resolution becomes a
runbook entry as the ticket closes, with an owner and a review date, so the
knowledge stops being personal property. That is an operating change, and AI
finally makes it cheap enough to hold.

### Post 05, Theme 2: Stop re-solving tickets the desk already solved

Under a fixed monthly fee, a recurring ticket is not a workload problem. It
is a pricing problem the client never sees.

The client pays the same whether their issue recurs four times or is fixed at
the root once. The MSP eats the difference, every month, for the life of the
agreement. Multiply by a book of clients and recurring tickets are one of the
quietest margin leaks in the model, booked as normal service, invisible in
every report that does not specifically hunt for them.

The counter is one metric and one discipline. The metric: recurring-ticket
rate per client, trending down or explained. The discipline: every recurrence
above a threshold triggers a root-cause decision by someone senior enough to
choose between fixing the environment, coaching the client, or repricing the
agreement. Those are three different answers to the same ticket, and only
judgment can pick between them.

### Post 06, Theme 2: Stop re-solving tickets the desk already solved

Watch what actually happens when a ticket escalates at most MSPs: the context
does not travel with it.

Tier three opens the ticket and finds a one-line description and a frustrated
client. What tier one tried, what the environment showed, which runbook was
followed and where it stopped matching reality, all of that lives in a chat
thread or nowhere. So the most expensive engineer in the building starts from
scratch, on a problem someone already spent two hours mapping.

The fix is structural: escalation carries evidence or it does not happen.
What was tried, what the system returned, which steps are ruled out. AI is
genuinely good at assembling that record from ticket history, and the payoff
lands at both ends: tier three starts from findings instead of from zero, and
tier one learns from watching what tier three does with them.

### Post 07, Theme 3: Improve delivery without loosening control

The most useful AI finding for MSP leaders was not measured on engineers at
all.

A field experiment with consultants at one elite firm found quality rose
sharply when AI was used on tasks inside its capability frontier, and fell
when people leaned on it for tasks outside that frontier (Dell'Acqua et al.,
2023; elite-firm setting, task-level results, and the frontier moves with the
model and the domain).

Now translate the failure mode to a service desk. Outside-frontier reliance
in consulting produces a weak slide. Outside-frontier reliance on an MSP desk
produces a plausible-sounding runbook action or security change, proposed
with confidence, against a live client environment. That is not a quality
problem. That is an incident with a head start.

Which is why the design answer on a desk is not enthusiasm or prohibition.
It is routing plus gates: AI drafts, retrieves, and summarizes freely; an
accountable engineer verifies against the live environment; and nothing
consequential reaches a client system without change approval. The gates are
not the tax on leverage. They are what makes the leverage safe to sell.

### Post 08, Theme 3: Improve delivery without loosening control

A QBR pack assembled the night before from ticketing exports has one job:
survive the meeting. A QBR built from live service data has a better one:
renew the contract.

Here is the failure I keep seeing. The pack says the quarter was fine. The
client remembers the outage, the migration that ran long, the ticket that
bounced three times. A polished deck that contradicts the client's lived
experience does not reassure them. It teaches them the reporting cannot be
trusted, and the renewal conversation inherits that lesson.

The fix is traceability plus honesty: every number drillable to the tickets
behind it, incident narratives that concede what went wrong and show what
changed, roadmap items tied to observed evidence from that client's own
environment. AI can assemble all of it from the service stack. The vCIO still
decides what leads, what gets conceded, and what the client hears first.
The client remembers the outage. If the QBR does not, the renewal will.

### Post 09, Theme 3: Improve delivery without loosening control

There is one rule I hold constant in every MSP AI conversation, at every
maturity stage, in every workflow: AI never holds standing permission to
change a client environment.

Draft the runbook entry, yes. Retrieve the past resolution, summarize the
ticket history, assemble the QBR, write up the change request with its
evidence attached. All of that is leverage, and all of it is safe precisely
because of what sits at the end of it: an accountable engineer, with a name,
who verifies against the live environment and owns the action.

The client did not hand their admin credentials to a toolchain. They handed
them to a firm, on the belief that a responsible person stands behind every
change. The moment that stops being literally true, the MSP is running a
different business than the one the client signed with, and the client just
has not found out yet.

### Post 10, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the desk down

"Governance will slow the desk down" assumes governance is a binder. It
should be rails.

The pattern set for an MSP fits on one page: client boundaries that mirror
the tenant isolation your stack already enforces. Role-based access that
mirrors the tier structure. A curated runbook corpus as the approved source,
so suggestions come from what the firm knows, not from the open internet's
guess. Credential vaulting, so secrets never enter a prompt. A human change
gate on every consequential action. Traceability riding along by default.

Every one of those buys speed as well as safety. An engineer who can see
where a suggestion came from verifies it in minutes. A desk with a sanctioned
path never needs the shadow one. And when a client's security questionnaire
asks how you use AI in their environment, the answer comes from the system,
not from a scramble.

### Post 11, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the desk down

The AI adoption surveys now agree on one shape, and it should worry anyone
running a service business: use is nearly universal, scaled value is not,
and most experiments never make it out of the pilot phase (McKinsey State of
AI 2025; Deloitte generative-AI survey 2025; self-reported consultancy
surveys, skewed to large enterprises, and not audited financials).

Access is not value capture. An assistant on every technician's screen is
the beginning of the story, not the result.

For an MSP the difference is measurement discipline, and the measures are
not AI metrics at all. Seats per engineer. First-touch resolution at tier
one. Mean time to resolution by ticket class. Recurring-ticket rate per
client. Onboarding days from contract to steady state. Effort versus
agreement value. Baseline them before the pilot, read them after, and let
the desk's own numbers decide what scales. Suggestion counts and adoption
percentages measure enthusiasm. These six measure the business.

### Post 12, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the desk down

You do not need an AI transformation. You need one honest test.

Pick two workflows, one from the service desk and one from the account side.
Ticket-to-Knowledge and Service-to-QBR Insight are the natural pair. Days 1
to 30: baseline them, set the client boundaries and credential rules, name
the owners, write the quality criteria and change gates where engineers will
see them. Days 31 to 60: run governed pilots with escalation tiers and change
approval unchanged, and instrument the desk numbers. Days 61 to 90: read the
results against the baseline, codify what worked into runbooks and defaults,
retire what did not, and brief leadership on evidence.

Ninety days later you know something true about your own desk, which is more
than most AI strategies ever deliver. And if the test says stop, you learned
that for the price of a pilot instead of a platform.
