## Campaign brief

**Target roles and personas.**
Primary: CEO / Founder (Growth Champion), COO / Managing Director (Efficiency
Driver), Executive Creative Director / Group Account Director (Client
Advocate). Secondary: Head of Technology (Technology Steward), Business
Affairs / Legal (Risk Guardian), CFO / Finance Director (Investment Skeptic).
Agency profile: independent and mid-size creative agencies where the founders
and senior creatives still touch the work.

**Core positioning.**
An agency sells ideas and execution against scopes that creep and retainers
that compress, and the product underneath is taste applied to someone else's
brand. Taste does not scale by hiring. Governed AI moves research, assembly,
versioning, and status synthesis onto rails so senior creative attention
returns to the idea, the room, and the call. Improved operations are the
product. AI is the enabling technology. Governance is why a client's brand,
their confidences, and the agency's claim to its own work survive the change,
because the creative call must stay human or the agency has no product.

**Trigger events.**
- A client or their procurement team asked, in a meeting or in a contract, whether and how AI touched their work.
- Senior creatives are producing versions instead of directing work, and the review calendar is the studio's bottleneck.
- The same credentials deck, case study, or boilerplate keeps getting rebuilt from scratch under pitch deadline.
- A retainer renewed at the same fee for visibly more scope, again.
- A write-off surfaced at reconciliation for a job everyone thought was fine.
- Usage rights for AI-assisted material came up in a contract negotiation and the agency's own paper was silent.

**Primary outcome.**
Booked Workflow Leverage & Trust Assessments with agencies matching the
profile. Secondary: playbook readership within target roles.

**Campaign themes.**
1. Grow without proportional hiring. 2. Stop recreating work.
3. Improve delivery without lowering quality. 4. Govern AI without slowing the firm down.

**Suggested publishing order.**
Rotate themes so no two consecutive posts share one: 01, 04, 07, 10, 02, 05,
08, 11, 03, 06, 09, 12. Two posts per week sustains the rotation for six weeks.

**Conversion path.**
Post → playbook (or persona page for targeted shares) → Workflow Leverage &
Trust Assessment → bounded pilot (two workflows, ninety days).

**Evidence discipline.**
Quantitative claims in posts come only from the social safe set in
`data/evidence/priority_claims_list.md` (section F), quoted with the caveat
language from `data/evidence/claim_cards.md`. The evidence ledger rejected
every public creative-agency utilization and realization benchmark it
reviewed, so no agency-economics numbers appear in this campaign; where the
gap matters, the posts say so plainly. Everything else stays qualitative.
Predictions are always labeled as predictions.

### Post 01, Theme 1: Grow without proportional hiring

Every agency growth conversation I have sat in eventually becomes a hiring
conversation, and it is usually the wrong one.

Desks were never the constraint. Senior creative attention is. The people
whose taste wins pitches and holds the bar take years to grow and cost a
fortune to buy, and every new win taxes the same few of them.

So before the next recruiting brief, run a cheaper experiment. Follow one
week of your senior creatives' hours and mark the ones only they could have
spent. Direction qualifies. Versioning, deck assembly, status compilation,
and reference hunting do not. The gap between those two numbers is capacity
the agency already owns. It is just being spent on the wrong work.

### Post 02, Theme 1: Grow without proportional hiring

Every agency I talk to is using AI. Almost none can show me where it landed
in the margin.

That gap is not an agency problem; it is everywhere. The 2025 enterprise
surveys report regular AI use at 88 percent of organizations in at least one
function, while only 39 percent report enterprise-level earnings impact, and
most experiments never fully scale (McKinsey State of AI 2025; Deloitte
generative-AI survey 2025). Both are self-reported consultancy surveys
skewed to large enterprises, not agency data and not audited financials,
which is exactly why I quote them with the label attached.

The pattern still teaches the right lesson: access is not value capture.
A studio full of subscriptions is not an operating model. The agencies that
will show AI in the margin are the ones that picked two workflows, baselined
them honestly, and measured what changed.

### Post 03, Theme 1: Grow without proportional hiring

I went looking for a defensible public benchmark on creative-agency
utilization and realization. I did not find one I would put my name on.

Plenty of numbers are in circulation. They trace to vendor blogs and
surveys without published methods, and when I ran them through the evidence
bar I use for client work, every one failed. So my agency playbook quotes no
agency-economics numbers at all, and says so on the first page.

That is not a hedge. It is the point. The only utilization number that
should drive your decisions is yours, and it takes about thirty days of
honest baselining to get it: revision rounds per deliverable, scope versus
actual by job, pitch cost, and where senior hours actually go. Borrowed
benchmarks are how agencies talk themselves into someone else's strategy.

### Post 04, Theme 2: Stop recreating work

It is 11pm before a pitch and someone is hunting through old decks for the
case study everyone remembers and nobody can find.

Every agency knows this night. The work exists, the results exist, the
credits exist, but they live in a finished PDF on a dead server, so the team
rebuilds the story from memory, at senior rates, slightly worse than last
time.

An agency's pitch advantage is what it has already made. A curated
case-study library, with an owner, provenance on every artifact, and client
permissions checked, turns that from folklore into infrastructure. First
drafts of credentials start from the agency's best, and the 11pm hunt
becomes a query. That is not an AI feature. It is an operating capability
that AI finally makes practical.

### Post 05, Theme 2: Stop recreating work

Watch what happens when someone new joins an account. They learn the
client's brand by getting it wrong once in front of them.

The guidelines are in a PDF somewhere. The voice lives in the current
team's heads. The reasons the last three campaigns worked, and the one idea
the CMO will never approve, are in nobody's file at all. So every staffing
change quietly resets the account's knowledge to zero and bills the client
for the relearning.

The fix is treating brand knowledge as an operating asset: guidelines,
voice, mandatories, approval politics, and campaign learnings held in a
governed corpus, walled to the account, current, and owned. New team members
start from the agency's accumulated understanding instead of from the PDF.
The client feels the difference in the first meeting.

### Post 06, Theme 2: Stop recreating work

Reuse has a failure mode that ends agency relationships: one client's
thinking surfacing in another client's work.

Most rosters hold competitors, or near enough. Point a retrieval tool at
the whole file server and it does not know that this positioning deck was
paid for by the brand across the aisle, that this insight came from a
confidential research debrief, or that these two clients must never share a
sentence. It will faithfully serve the agency's memory to the wrong room, at
machine speed.

So the reuse capability worth building has three properties: curated
sources rather than the pile, account walls that mirror team assignment, and
provenance on every artifact so a reviewer can see where each piece came
from before it ships. Craft patterns travel between accounts. A client's
brand thinking never does. Leverage and trust are the same build.

### Post 07, Theme 3: Improve delivery without lowering quality

The best causal evidence we have on AI and professional work is worth
reading precisely, especially in a creative business.

In a preregistered experiment published in Science, access to a general AI
assistant cut time on occupation-specific professional writing tasks by
about 40 percent and raised judged quality by about 18 percent, across
roughly 453 college-educated professionals (Noy and Zhang, 2023). Note what
was measured: controlled writing tasks, evaluator-scored. Production-adjacent
work. Not creative concepting, not client engagements, and not agency margin.

Nobody has credibly measured what AI does to the quality of an idea. That
gap should shape your operating model: aim the leverage at the mechanical
layer, research, first-pass copy, versions, adaptations, where the evidence
lives, and keep the concept and the call entirely human, where it does not.

### Post 08, Theme 3: Improve delivery without lowering quality

The same field experiment that measured big gains from AI inside its
capability frontier found something agencies should tattoo on the studio
wall: quality fell when professionals leaned on it for tasks outside that
frontier (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023). That was consulting tasks at one elite
firm, so the transfer to creative work is an inference, and the frontier
itself moves as models change. Which is exactly why the finding matters as a
design principle rather than a statistic.

In an agency, the outside-frontier failure has a specific face: derivative
territory, category sameness, or work a client cannot own, sold as original.
Generative tools average. Lean on them for the idea itself and your concepts
converge on whatever every competitor's tools also reach.

The design answer is routing plus review. Machines carry research,
variants, and versions. Ideas and the call stay human, and everything passes
the same creative review gate it always did.

### Post 09, Theme 3: Improve delivery without lowering quality

Version chaos is not a client problem. It is an approvals design problem,
and AI is about to make it better or much worse.

Here is the failure I keep seeing: six stakeholders comment, someone asks a
model to consolidate the feedback, and it produces a smooth, confident
summary of a disagreement. The studio revises toward a position nobody
actually holds. Then revises again when the disagreement resurfaces. More
rounds, faster, is not progress.

Consolidation has to preserve the conflict, not average it away: whose
comment is whose, where they collide, and a named decider on the client side
who resolves it. One feedback channel, one version log both sides can see,
internal creative review before anything external. The revision count drops
because the process stops manufacturing revisions.

Averaged feedback is not consensus. It is a new opinion nobody holds.

### Post 10, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

Who owns AI-assisted work is no longer a philosophical question. It is a
contractual one, and agencies should know what the strongest primary
guidance actually says.

In January 2025 the US Copyright Office concluded that using AI tools to
assist rather than replace human creativity does not, by itself, deny
copyright protection for the original human expression in a work (Copyright
and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2). Read it precisely: it is agency
analysis, not a court holding, it leaves training-data and infringement
questions to other proceedings, and it is not legal advice for your client
contracts. Get your own counsel on your own paper.

The operational lesson survives the caveats: protection attaches to the
human contribution. Which means documenting human creative direction on
every deliverable is not bureaucracy. It is how the agency supports its
rights posture and its disclosure answer with the same habit.

### Post 11, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

Sooner or later a client is going to ask your agency whether AI touched
their work. The dangerous version is when three people from the same agency
give three different answers.

The client is not really asking whether you used AI. They are asking
whether you are in control of how. An improvised answer, different by
account team and by meeting, tells them you are not, and that discovery does
more damage than any honest disclosure ever would.

The fix costs one working session: write the posture down with business
affairs. What AI may touch and what it may never touch. How human creative
direction is documented. What the walls between accounts are. What the
agency says when asked, in plain language, given the same way by everyone.
Agencies that do this stop dreading the question. Some start bringing it up
first, because a controlled answer is a trust asset.

### Post 12, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

Your agency does not need an AI transformation. It needs one honest test.

Pick two workflows, one from winning work and one from the studio. Days 1
to 30: baseline them honestly, revision rounds, scope versus actual, pitch
cost included, set the account boundaries and approved libraries, and write
the usage-rights and disclosure rules with business affairs. Days 31 to 60:
run governed pilots with creative review discipline unchanged, and
instrument the numbers. Days 61 to 90: read the results against the
baseline, codify what worked into defaults, retire what did not, and brief
leadership on evidence.

Ninety days later you know something true about your own shop, which is
more than most AI strategies ever deliver. And if the test says stop, you
learned that for the price of a pilot instead of a program.
