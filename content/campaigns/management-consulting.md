## Campaign brief

**Target roles and personas.**
Primary: Managing Partner / CEO (Growth Champion), COO / Operations Director
(Efficiency Driver), Practice Leader / Engagement Partner (Client Advocate).
Secondary: CIO / IT Director (Technology Steward), General Counsel / Risk Lead
(Risk Guardian), CFO / Finance Partner (Investment Skeptic). Firm profile:
mid-size management consulting firms where partners still touch delivery.

**Core positioning.**
Consulting firms grow on senior attention, and senior attention does not
scale by hiring. Governed AI moves assembly, drafting, retrieval, and
synthesis onto rails so partner hours return to promise, price, and judgment.
Improved operations are the product. AI is the enabling technology.
Governance is why the result can be trusted with client work.

**Trigger events.**
- The growth plan requires hiring the market will not supply at the price the model affords.
- Partners are at capacity while the pipeline is still growing.
- The same proposal, framework, or model keeps getting rebuilt from memory.
- A client asked, in a meeting or in a contract, how the firm uses AI.
- Leadership pressure to "do something with AI" with no operating answer behind it.
- Margin compression that utilization alone no longer explains.

**Primary outcome.**
Booked Workflow Leverage & Trust Assessments with firms matching the
profile. Secondary: playbook readership within target roles.

**Campaign themes.**
1. Grow without proportional hiring. 2. Stop recreating work.
3. Improve delivery without lowering quality. 4. Govern AI without slowing the firm down.

**Suggested publishing order.**
Rotate themes so no two consecutive posts share one: 01, 04, 07, 10, 02, 05,
08, 11, 03, 06, 09, 12. Two posts per week sustains the rotation for six weeks.

**Conversion path.**
Post → playbook (or persona page for targeted shares) → Workflow Leverage &
Trust Assessment → bounded pilot (two workflows, ninety days).

**Evidence discipline.**
Exact figures in posts come only from claims holding Approved status under
`research/evidence-policy.md`. Verified claims may appear only as attributed
directional statements, with the source name and its caveat adjacent, and
never with a figure attached. Candidate and rejected claims do not appear at
all. The section F "social safe set" in
`data/evidence/priority_claims_list.md` predates the WS-19 validation pass
and is no longer authoritative on its own. Everything else stays
qualitative. Predictions are always labeled as predictions.

### Post 01, Theme 1: Grow without proportional hiring

Every consulting growth plan I have seen lately has the same silent
assumption buried in it: that the firm can hire senior people as fast as it
can win work.

It cannot. Partners who can win work, shape it, and stand behind it take
years to grow and cost a fortune to buy. Desks and analysts were never the
constraint. Senior attention is.

Which means the real growth question is not "how do we hire faster." It is
"how much of what our senior people do this week actually requires them."
Follow one week of partner hours and mark the ones only that partner could
have spent. The rest is assembly: finding, drafting, formatting,
reconstructing. That is the capacity hiding in the firm you already have.

### Post 02, Theme 1: Grow without proportional hiring

Revenue per professional is the most honest number in a consulting firm, and
the least discussed.

Headcount growth flatters everything: bigger teams, bigger logos, bigger
league-table position. Revenue per professional tells you whether the firm is
becoming more valuable or just bigger. When it is flat while headcount
climbs, the firm is buying growth with margin.

The way to move it is not to work people harder. It is to stop paying senior
rates for work that is really retrieval and assembly: the proposal rebuilt
from memory, the framework reconstructed from a deck nobody can find, the
status pack assembled by hand every Friday. Move that work onto governed
rails and the ratio starts to move, without a single additional hire.

There is a version of this you can check before the next partner meeting.
Put revenue per professional next to headcount for the last three years and
see which line grew faster. If headcount won, the firm has been buying
growth, and no industry benchmark is going to tell you more than that
comparison already does.

### Post 03, Theme 1: Grow without proportional hiring

"We need to hire ahead of the revenue" is how consulting firms have always
grown, and it quietly prices a bet: that utilization arrives before the
payroll does.

There is a version of capacity that does not require the bet. Delivery hours
are not all the same hour. Some of them are recoverable: time spent
recreating material the firm already produced, hunting for the person who
did this before, assembling status from scattered sources. None of that is
the judgment clients pay for. All of it competes with the judgment for the
same calendar.

I have not seen a credible public benchmark for how large that share runs,
and I am not going to tell you what it is in your firm, because I have not
measured your firm. That is the point: baseline two workflows for ninety

### Post 04, Theme 2: Stop recreating work

Ask a partner where the firm's best proposal from last year is. Not the
final PDF. The thinking: the pricing logic, the team structure, the win
themes.

In most firms the honest answer is "in the head of whoever wrote it," and
that person may have left. So the next pursuit team rebuilds it, at senior
rates, on a deadline, slightly worse.

A consulting firm's edge is what it has already learned. A firm that cannot
retrieve its own best work is renting its memory from whoever happens to
remember. Retrieval with provenance, walled by engagement, reviewed before
reuse: that is not an AI feature. It is an operating capability that AI
finally makes practical.

### Post 05, Theme 2: Stop recreating work

Lessons learned is the most abandoned deliverable in consulting. Everyone
means to write it. The next engagement starts Monday.

The cost is invisible because it books as normal delivery: the mistake
repeated with a new client, the approach reinvented by a new team, the
expert rediscovered by accident at the coffee machine. The firm pays for the
same learning twice and calls it utilization.

The fix is not discipline. Discipline loses to Monday every time. The fix is
making the close-out a governed workflow: the write-back happens as part of
shipping, it lands in a curated corpus with an owner and a review date, and
the next engagement starts from it by default. Memory as infrastructure, not
virtue.

### Post 06, Theme 2: Stop recreating work

Reuse has a failure mode nobody puts on the slide: the fastest way to end a
client relationship is to let another client's material surface in their
deliverable.

Point a model at the whole file share and that risk is now running at
machine speed. The share contains superseded frameworks, drafts that were
wrong, and confidential context from every client the firm has ever served.
Retrieval does not know which is which unless the firm tells it.

So the reuse capability that actually compounds has three properties:
curated sources, not the pile. Engagement boundaries, so client walls hold.
Provenance on every artifact, so a reviewer can see where each piece came
from before it ships. Leverage and trust are the same build.

### Post 07, Theme 3: Improve delivery without lowering quality

The fear in every partner meeting about AI is the same fear, differently
dressed: that speed will be bought with quality, and the firm's name will
pay for it.

Field evidence says both halves of that fear are real. In an experiment at
one elite consulting firm, AI was reported to raise judged quality on tasks
inside its capability frontier, and to lower it when consultants leaned on
the tool outside that frontier (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023; elite-firm setting,
task-level results, not mid-market ROI). The design conclusion is not
enthusiasm or prohibition. It is routing plus review.

Here is the design answer. Nothing about review changes. The partner still
reviews promise, price, and team on every proposal. The engagement lead
still reads every client-facing document. What changes is what arrives at
the review: a draft grounded in the firm's own best work, with provenance
attached, instead of a blank-page assembly finished at midnight.

Review discipline is not the tax on capacity. It is what makes the capacity
sellable. A faster draft that still passes the same gate is pure gain.

### Post 08, Theme 3: Improve delivery without lowering quality

A steering committee is only as good as its ability to challenge a number,
and most engagement status packs cannot survive the challenge. The figure
was assembled by hand on Friday from memory and optimism. Ask where it came
from and the room goes quiet.

Synthesis helps, and synthesis is also the trap: a model summarizing status
reports will smooth a slipping engagement into confident prose. The fix is
not better summaries. It is traceability: every number in the pack drills to
the artifact behind it in one step, one source of record per metric, a named
owner on every escalated risk.

A status number you cannot trace is a rumor wearing a format. Partners
deserve instruments, not performances.

### Post 09, Theme 3: Improve delivery without lowering quality

The old client-communication failure was the update that never went out. The
new one is worse: the update that went out with nobody home.

AI drafts a competent weekly update in seconds. Send it unread and
eventually it will carry a wrong name, a tone-deaf line, or last week's
numbers, and the client will learn something corrosive: nobody here is
actually paying attention. Trust that took a year to build can be spent in
one template.

The rule in every governed rollout I run is boring and absolute: a human
reads, edits, and owns every client-facing message. No exceptions for the
routine ones, because clients cannot tell which ones you considered routine.
Drafted by a machine is fine. Sent unread is not.

### Post 10, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

"Governance will slow us down" assumes governance is a committee. It should
be rails.

The pattern set for a consulting firm fits on one page: engagement
boundaries that decide what AI can reach. Role-based access that mirrors
staffing. Approved sources that decide what drafts are built from.
Provenance that rides along automatically. Human review before anything
client-facing. Named approval points. Retention and reuse rules set at
engagement close.

Every one of those buys speed as well as safety. A reviewer who can see
where each piece came from reviews in minutes. A consultant with a
sanctioned path never needs the shadow one. The firms that move fastest on
AI are the ones whose people never have to stop and wonder whether they are
allowed to.

### Post 11, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

Somewhere in your next partner meeting about AI, the general counsel is
going to ask the confidentiality question. The program that survives is the
one that prepared the answer before the meeting.

Six chairs hold a veto on any real change in a consulting firm: managing
partner, COO, practice leaders, CIO, risk or general counsel, CFO. Each asks
a different question. Growth without dilution. Reliability. Quality. One
platform, not thirty tools. Client confidentiality and accountability. Cost
and return. Champion-led AI initiatives die because they answer one chair
brilliantly and meet the other five unprepared.

Design for all six from the start and governance stops being the obstacle.
It becomes the reason the partnership says yes.

### Post 12, Theme 4: Govern AI without slowing the firm down

You do not need an AI transformation. You need one honest test.

Pick two workflows, one from business development and one from delivery.
Days 1 to 30: baseline them, set the boundaries and approved sources, name
the owners, write the quality criteria where reviewers will see them. Days
31 to 60: run governed pilots with review discipline unchanged, and
instrument the numbers. Days 61 to 90: read the results against the
baseline, codify what worked into defaults, retire what did not, and brief
the partnership on evidence.

Ninety days later you know something true about your own firm, which is
more than most AI strategies ever deliver. And if the test says stop, you
learned that for the price of a pilot instead of a program.
